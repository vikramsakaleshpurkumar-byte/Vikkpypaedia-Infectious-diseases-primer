<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

People acutely infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) may be asymptomatic or symptomatic. The likelihood of developing symptoms increases with age: less than 1% of infants younger than 1 year, 5% to 15% of children 1 through 5 years, and 30% to 50% of people 6 through 30 years of age are symptomatic. Few data are available for adults older than 30 years.

</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

HBV has a partially double-stranded DNA genome contained within a 42-nm-diameter enveloped virion and belongs in the family Hepadnaviridae. Important components of the viral particle include an outer lipoprotein envelope containing HBsAg, an inner nucleocapsid consisting of hepatitis B core antigen (HBcAg), and the viral polymerase attached to the viral genome.

*Printed p. 347 / PDF p. 361*
</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

HBV is transmitted through infected blood or body fluids. Although HBsAg has been detected in multiple body fluids including human milk, saliva, and tears, the most potentially infectious fluids include blood, serum, semen, vaginal secretions, and cerebrospinal, synovial, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal, and amniotic fluids. People with chronic HBV infection are the primary reservoirs for infection.

*Printed p. 347 / PDF p. 361*
</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Serologic protein antigen tests are available commercially to detect HBsAg and HBeAg. Serologic antibody assays also are available for detection of anti-HBs, total anti-HBc, IgM anti-HBc, and anti- HBe . Most laboratories now use nucleic acid amplification tests for analysis of HBV DNA, with very high sensitivity at low levels and broad dynamic range for quantitation.

*Printed p. 348 / PDF p. 362*
</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

No therapy for uncomplicated acute HBV infection is recommended. Treatment with a nucleoside or nucleotide analogue is indicated if there is concern for severe infection with acute liver failure. Acute HBV infection may be difficult to distinguish from reactivation of HBV.

*Printed p. 350 / PDF p. 364*
</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for infection prevention and control measures in health care settings.</summary>

Standard precautions are indicated for patients with acute or chronic HBV infection. For infants whose birthing parent is HBsAg positive, no special care in addition to standard precautions, as described under Control Measures, is needed, other than removal of birthing parent’s blood by a gloved attendant.

*Printed p. 351 / PDF p. 365*
</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin3 HBIG provides short-term protection (3–6 months) and is indicated in specific postexposure circumstances. HBIG is prepared from the plasma of donors with high concentrations of anti-HBs, with an anti-HBs titer of at least 1:100,000 by radioimmunoassay. Standard immunoglobulin is not effective for postexposure prophylaxis against HBV infection, because concentrations of anti-HBs are too low.

*Printed p. 351 / PDF p. 365*
</details>

<details><summary>Hepatitis B: recall the key points for special considerations.</summary>

Considerations for High-Risk Groups Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis Immunization is recommended for susceptible patients undergoing hemodialysis. Immunization early in the course of renal disease is encouraged, because response is better than in advanced disease. Specific dosage recommendations have not been made for children undergoing hemodialysis.
