# Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice) — active recall

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Itching is the most common symptom of head lice infestation, but many children are asymptomatic. Adult lice (2–3 mm long, tan to grayish white, with claws on all 6 legs) or eggs (match hair color) and nits (empty egg casings, white) are found on the hair and are most readily apparent behind the ears and near the nape of the neck. Excoriations and crusting caused by secondary bacterial infection may occur and often are associated with regional lymphadenopathy.

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

Pediculus humanus capitis is the head louse. Both nymphs and adult lice feed on human blood.

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Head lice infestation in the United States is most common in children attending child care, preschool, and elementary school. It is not a sign of poor hygiene, and all socioeconomic groups are affected. Head lice infestation is not influenced by hair length, hair texture, or frequency of shampooing or brushing.

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Identification of eggs, nymphs, and adult lice with the naked eye is possible; diagnosis can be confirmed by using a hand lens, dermatoscope (epiluminescence microscope), or traditional microscope. Nymphal and adult lice shun light and move rapidly to conceal themselves. Wetting the hair with water, oil, or a conditioner to “slow down” the movement of the lice and using a fine-tooth comb may improve ability to diagnose infestation and shorten inspection time.

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Treatment should be initiated only if there is a diagnosis of active head lice infestation. A number of effective pediculicidal agents are available to treat head lice infestation . Costs and recommended age ranges vary by product.

Standard precautions plus contact precautions are recommended until the patient has been treated with an appropriate pediculicide.

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Household and other close contacts should be examined and treated if infested. Bedmates of infested people should be treated prophylactically at the same time as the infested household members and contacts, even if bedmates do not have identifiable live lice. Children should not be excluded or sent home early from school or child care because of head lice, because head lice have a low contagion in these settings.

<details><summary>Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): recall the key points for treatment — additional detail.</summary>

Therapy can be initiated with over-the-counter 1% permethrin lotion or with pyrethrin combined with piperonyl butoxide, both of which have good safety profiles. Prevalence of clinical resistance to these products is highly variable from community to community and country to country.
