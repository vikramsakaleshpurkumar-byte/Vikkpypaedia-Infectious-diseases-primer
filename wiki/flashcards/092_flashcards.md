<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

The presentation of mpox (formerly known as monkeypox) is similar to but milder than that of smallpox, prior to its eradication.

*Printed p. 586 / PDF p. 600*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

Monkeypox virus, which causes mpox disease, is a member of the Poxviridae family (genus Orthopoxvirus). Other members of this genus that can infect humans include cowpox virus, vaccinia virus, and smallpox (variola).

*Printed p. 586 / PDF p. 600*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Clade I (previously called the Congo Basin clade of mpox virus) is associated with an 11% case fatality rate in Africa in individuals without prior smallpox vaccination.

*Printed p. 586 / PDF p. 600*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Mpox virus can be detected in a swab of a lesion or a lesion crust or exudate (unroofing or aspirating a lesion is not recommended) by Orthopoxvirus or mpox-specific nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs). Diagnostic testing is available through the U.S. Laboratory Response Network, through consultation with state or local public health departments, and through some commercial laboratories.

*Printed p. 587 / PDF p. 601*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Most patients with mpox have self-limited disease. Patients should be counseled not to touch their eyes. Patients with ocular or periorbital lesions should be treated promptly because of the risk of developing keratitis and potentially permanent visual problems; these patients should have a consultation with an ophthalmologist regarding management and discussion of ocular therapy, such as topical trifluridine.

*Printed p. 587 / PDF p. 601*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for infection prevention and control measures in health care settings.</summary>

A patient suspected of having mpox should be placed in a private room, and a health care professional should use a National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health-approved particulate respirator equipped with an N95 or higher respirator, gown, gloves, and eye protection. If an aerosol-generating procedure is performed, the patient should be placed in a private room equipped with negative-pressure ventilation with high-efficiency particulate air filtration. Good hand hygiene should be practiced, and extreme care should be taken with materials that may have been contaminated by the patient’s lesions, crusts, or body fluids, so as not to create aerosols or contaminate additional items.

*Printed p. 588 / PDF p. 602*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Preexposure Immunization Laboratory workers handling mpox specimens or a member of a high-risk group, as identified by public health, may be eligible for vaccination and for booster doses. Recommendations from the CDC Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) are available.2 JYNNEOS is a live, nonreplicating vaccinia vaccine that was approved by the FDA in 2019 for the prevention of mpox and smallpox in persons 18 years or older who are at high risk for mpox. It is given as a 2-dose series, separated by 28 days.

*Printed p. 589 / PDF p. 603*
</details>

<details><summary>Mpox: recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

Clinical Presentation Associated With Classic Mpox Virus Infections Classic mpox virus infection generally involves a prodromal illness that may last several days and is characterized by fever, lymphadenopathy, malaise, sore throat, and headache, followed by the development of a characteristic diffuse rash. The rash typically involves many lesions, begins on the face, and spreads to other parts of the body including the extremities (often with involvement of palms and soles) and trunk.
