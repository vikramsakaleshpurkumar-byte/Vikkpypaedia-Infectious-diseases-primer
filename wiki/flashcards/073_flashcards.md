<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Most human papillomavirus (HPV) infections are subclinical, and 90% spontaneously become undetectable within 2 years. However, persistent HPV infection can cause benign epithelial proliferation (warts) of the skin and mucous membranes as well as cancers of the lower anogenital tract and the oropharynx. HPV types can be grouped into cutaneous and mucosal types.

<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

HPVs are small, nonenveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses of the Papillomaviridae family, which are grouped into genera, species, and types based on DNA sequence variation. More than 200 types are recognized, but specific tissue tropism and disease associations are limited to date to less than 40 types. Types 6 and 11 cause anogenital warts (condylomata acuminata), RRP, and conjunctival papillomas but rarely are found in cancer.

<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Virtually all adults will be infected with some type of HPV during their lives. In the United States, it is estimated that 42 million people are infected with disease-associated HPV and 13 million people acquire a new anogenital HPV infection each year. Although HPV is common in the general population, specific risk factors include higher lifetime number of sex partners, earlier age at first sex, and immunocompromising conditions, including HIV infection.

<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Most cutaneous and anogenital warts can be diagnosed through clinical inspection. Serologic testing for HPV antibodies does not inform clinical decisions and is not commercially available. HPVassociated cervical lesions are detected through screening methods (described below).

<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

There is no FDA-approved treatment for HPV infection. Treatment may be directed toward lesions caused by HPV.

<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for control measures and care of exposed people.</summary>

Sexual abuse should be considered if anogenital warts are found in a child. When anogenital warts are found in a child who is prepubertal, the patient should be referred to a child abuse management pediatrician for an abuse evaluation. Suspected child sexual abuse should be reported to the appropriate local agency.

<details><summary>Human Papillomaviruses: recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

Warts are common, benign lesions, although they may be associated with significant clinical problems. Common skin warts are dome-shaped with conical projections that give the surface a rough appearance.
