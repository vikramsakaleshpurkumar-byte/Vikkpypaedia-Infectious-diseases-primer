<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Pertussis begins with mild upper respiratory tract symptoms similar to the common cold (catarrhal stage) and progresses to cough, usually paroxysms of cough (paroxysmal stage), characterized by inspiratory whoop (gasping) after repeated cough on the same breath, which commonly is followed by post-tussive emesis. Symptoms wane gradually over weeks to months (convalescent stage). Cough illness in immunized children and adults can range from typical to very mild.

</details>

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

Pertussis is caused by a fastidious, gram-negative, pleomorphic bacillus, Bordetella pertussis. Other Bordetella species can cause sporadic prolonged cough illness in people, including Bordetella parapertussis, Bordetella bronchiseptica (the cause of canine kennel cough), and Bordetella holmesii.

</details>

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Humans are the only known hosts of B pertussis. Transmission occurs by close contact with infected individuals via large respiratory droplets generated by coughing or sneezing. Cases occur year-round, typically with a late summer-autumn peak.

*Printed p. 667 / PDF p. 681*
</details>

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Culture previously was considered the gold standard for laboratory diagnosis of pertussis but is not optimally sensitive and has largely been replaced by nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs). Culture requires collection of an appropriate nasopharyngeal swab specimen, obtained either by aspiration or with polyester or flocked rayon swabs. Specimens must not be allowed to dry during prompt transport to the laboratory.

*Printed p. 667 / PDF p. 681*
</details>

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Antimicrobial therapy administered during the catarrhal stage may ameliorate the disease. Antimicrobial therapy is indicated before test results are received if the clinical history is strongly suggestive of pertussis or the patient is at high risk of severe or complicated disease (eg, an infant). A 5-day course of azithromycin is the appropriate first-line choice for treatment and for postexposure prophylaxis .

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for infection prevention and control measures in health care settings.</summary>

In addition to standard precautions, droplet precautions are recommended for 21 days from onset of cough if appropriate antimicrobial therapy is not administered or for 5 days after initiation of effective therapy.

*Printed p. 669 / PDF p. 683*
</details>

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Care of Exposed People Individuals, regardless of immunization status and in all settings, who have been in close contact with a person infected with pertussis should be monitored closely for respiratory tract symptoms for 21 days after last contact with the infected person. Close contacts with cough should be evaluated. People in child care settings (both workers and children), schools (both teachers and students), and health care settings with confirmed pertussis should be excluded from child care, school, or work until completion of 5 days of the recommended course of antimicrobial therapy.

</details>

<details><summary>Pertussis (Whooping Cough): recall the key points for diagnostic tests — additional detail.</summary>

Food and Drug Administration (FDA), including polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays, are commercially available as stand-alone tests or as multiplex assays and are the most commonly used laboratory method for detection of B pertussis because of greater sensitivity and more rapid turnaround time. The PCR test requires collection of an adequate nasopharyngeal specimen using a polyester or flocked swab or nasopharyngeal wash or aspirate.
