<details><summary>Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcal) Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a common bacterial cause of acute otitis media (AOM), sinusitis, community-acquired pneumonia, and pediatric conjunctivitis; pleural empyema, mastoiditis, and periorbital cellulitis also occur. It is the most common cause of bacterial meningitis in infants and children ages 2 months to 11 years in the United States. S pneumoniae also may cause endocarditis, pericarditis, peritonitis, pyogenic arthritis, osteomyelitis, soft-tissue infection, and neonatal septicemia.

<details><summary>Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcal) Infections: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

S pneumoniae organisms (pneumococci) are lancet-shaped, gram-positive, catalase-negative diplococci. More than 100 pneumococcal serotypes have been identified on the basis of unique polysaccharide capsules.

<details><summary>Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcal) Infections: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Nasopharyngeal carriage rates in children range from 21% in industrialized settings to more than 90% in resource-limited settings. Transmission is from person to person by respiratory droplet contact. Viral upper respiratory tract infections, including influenza and respiratory syncytial virus, can predispose to pneumococcal infection and transmission.

<details><summary>Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcal) Infections: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Recovery of S pneumoniae from a normally sterile site confirms the diagnosis. The finding of lancetshaped gram-positive organisms and white blood cells in expectorated sputum (older children and adults) or pleural exudate suggests pneumococcal pneumonia. Recovery of pneumococci by culture of an upper respiratory tract swab specimen is not sufficient to assign an etiologic diagnosis of pneumococcal disease involving the middle ear, upper or lower respiratory tract, or sinus.

<details><summary>Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcal) Infections: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Bacterial Meningitis Possibly or Proven to Be Caused by S pneumoniae For children with bacterial meningitis possibly or known to be caused by S pneumoniae, vancomycin should be administered in addition to third-generation cephalosporin because of the possibility of S pneumoniae organisms that are nonsusceptible to penicillin and third-generation cephalosporins. In neonates, when cefotaxime is not available then ceftazidime or cefepime can be used in addition to vancomycin. Vancomycin should be stopped if susceptibility to thirdgeneration cephalosporins is documented , if another organism not requiring vancomycin is identified, or if the CSF culture is negative.

<details><summary>Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcal) Infections: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Active Immunization Four pneumococcal vaccines are available for use in children in the United States: 3 pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV13, PCV15, and PCV20), and 1 pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (the 23-valent PPSV23). PCV13, PCV15, and PCV20 are licensed for use in infants and children 6 weeks and older as well as in adults. PCV13 is composed of 13 purified capsular polysaccharide serotypes (1, 3, 4, 5, 6A, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 18C, 19A, 19F, and 23F).
