<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

The most common group A streptococcal (GAS) infection is acute pharyngotonsillitis (pharyngitis), which manifests as sore throat with tonsillar inflammation and often tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, palatal petechiae, or a strawberry tongue. Purulent complications of pharyngitis include peritonsillar or retropharyngeal abscesses, suppurative cervical adenitis, and rarely, sinusitis and otitis media. Nonsuppurative complications include acute rheumatic fever (ARF) and acute glomerulonephritis (AGN).

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

More than 240 distinct serotypes or genotypes of group A streptococci (Streptococcus pyogenes) have been identified based on M protein serotype or M protein gene sequence (emm types). In general, emm typing is more discriminating than M protein serotyping. Epidemiologic studies indicate an association between certain emm types (eg, types 1, 3, 5, 6, 14, 18, 19, and 24) and rheumatic fever, but a specific rheumatogenic factor remains unidentified.

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Pharyngitis usually results from respiratory droplet transmission but can also occur through contact with respiratory secretions or wound discharge from an infected person. Although environmental transmission via surfaces and fomites was historically not believed to occur, evidence from outbreak investigations indicates that environmental transmission of GAS can occur. Household pets, such as dogs, are not vectors.

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Testing for Group A Streptococci in Pharyngitis Children with acute onset of sore throat and clinical signs and symptoms such as pharyngeal exudate, enlarged tonsils, pain on swallowing, fever, and enlarged tender anterior cervical nodes are more likely to have GAS infection and should be tested. Children with pharyngitis and obvious viral features (eg, rhinorrhea, cough, hoarseness, oral ulcers) should not be tested or treated for GAS infection. Testing also is generally not recommended for children younger than 3 years, although testing and treatment may be considered for some symptomatic children younger than 3 years who have other risk factors, such as an older sibling with GAS infection.

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

S pyogenes is uniformly susceptible to all b-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins); thus, susceptibility testing is needed only for non– b-lactam agents, such as a macrolide or clindamycin, to which some strains of S pyogenes are resistant.

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for infection prevention and control measures in health care settings.</summary>

In addition to standard precautions, droplet precautions are recommended for those with GAS pharyngitis or pneumonia until 24 hours after initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy. For burns with secondary GAS infection and extensive or draining cutaneous infections that cannot be covered or contained adequately by dressings, contact precautions should be used until at least 24 hours after initiation of appropriate therapy.

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

The most important means of controlling GAS disease and its sequelae is prompt identification and treatment of infections.

<details><summary>Group A Streptococcal Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

Scarlet fever occurs most often with pharyngitis and, rarely, with pyoderma or an infected wound. Scarlet fever involves a characteristic confluent erythematous sandpaper-like rash caused by one or more GAS erythrogenic exotoxins.
