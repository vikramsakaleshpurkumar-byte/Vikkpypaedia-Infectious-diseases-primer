<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Rocky Mountain spotted fever (RMSF) is a systemic, predominantly small-vessel vasculitis that often involves a characteristic rash. Unlike many other spotted fever rickettsioses, eschars at sites of tick bites do not develop with RMSF. High fever, myalgia, headache (less commonly reported in young children), nausea, vomiting, and malaise are typical presenting symptoms.

</details>

<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

Rickettsia rickettsii, an obligate, intracellular, gram-negative bacillus and a member of the spotted fever group of rickettsiae, is the causative agent of RMSF. The primary targets of infection in mammalian hosts are endothelial cells lining the small blood vessels of all major tissues and organs. Diffuse small vessel vasculitis and vascular injury leads to increased permeability and tissue edema.

</details>

<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

The pathogen is transmitted to humans by the bites of several genera of hard ticks of the family Ixodidae. The principal recognized vectors of R rickettsii are Dermacentor variabilis (the American dog tick) in the eastern and central United States and Dermacentor andersoni (the Rocky Mountain wood tick) in the northern and western United States. Rhipicephalus sanguineus (the brown dog tick) has been confirmed as a vector of R rickettsii in Arizona and Mexico and may play a role in other regions.

</details>

<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

The diagnosis of RMSF must be made using clinical signs and symptoms and can be confirmed later using diagnostic tests. Treatment should never be delayed while awaiting laboratory confirmation or because of lack of history of tick bite; approximately half of RMSF cases occur in people who do not report a tick bite. The indirect immunofluorescence antibody assay using R rickettsii antigen is considered the reference standard serologic assay.

*Printed p. 760 / PDF p. 774*
</details>

<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Doxycycline is the drug of choice for treatment of RMSF in patients of any age and should be started as soon as RMSF is suspected. Physicians should treat empirically if RMSF is being considered and should not postpone treatment while awaiting laboratory confirmation. Treatment is most effective if initiated in the first 5 days of symptoms, and treatment started after that time is less likely to prevent death or other adverse outcomes.

<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Limiting exposures to ticks and tick bites is the primary means of prevention. Prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents to prevent RMSF is not recommended, even in children with a documented tick bite who are asymptomatic. People who were bitten by a tick in an area with endemic disease should be advised to see a health care provider should they develop signs or symptoms of RMSF.

<details><summary>Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever: recall the key points for diagnostic tests1 — additional detail.</summary>

RMSF can be diagnosed by detection of R rickettsii DNA in acute whole blood, tissue, and serum specimens by nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs). R rickettsii typically does not circulate in large numbers in whole blood until advanced stages of disease; assays relying on detection of DNA can lack sensitivity, and a negative result does not rule out RMSF.
