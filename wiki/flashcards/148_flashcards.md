<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Tinea cruris is a common superficial fungal disorder of the groin, pubic/perianal area, suprapubic abdomen, and upper thighs. It is more common in male adults and adolescents and uncommon in prepubertal children.1 The lesions often are ringshaped or circular (hence, the lay term “ringworm”), are sharply marginated, and can be intensely pruritic (jock itch). The involved skin is slightly erythematous and scaly, with color variations from red to brown.

<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

Tinea cruris develops when dermatophyte fungi invade the outer skin layers of the affected body region. The fungi Epidermophyton floccosum, Trichophyton rubrum, and Trichophyton mentagrophytes are the most common causes. Trichophyton tonsurans, Trichophyton verrucosum, and Trichophyton interdigitale also have been identified as causes.

<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Tinea cruris occurs predominantly in male adolescents and adults and is acquired principally through indirect contact with desquamated epithelium or hair. Direct person-to-person transmission also occurs. Moisture, close-fitting garments, noncotton underwear, friction, and obesity are predisposing factors.

<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Treatment is similar to that for tinea corporis. Treatment of concurrent onychomycosis (tinea unguium) and tinea pedis may reduce recurrence. Recurrence is common, particularly if predisposing factors such as moisture and friction are not minimized.

<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Infections should be treated promptly. Involved areas should be kept dry to prevent recurrences, and antifungal powders and wearing loose-fitting undergarments may be useful. Patients should be advised to dry the groin area before drying their feet to avoid inoculating dermatophytes of tinea pedis into the groin area.

<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

The differential diagnosis for tinea cruris includes intertrigo, candidiasis, psoriasis (particularly inverse psoriasis), other dermatitides (seborrheic, atopic, irritant or allergic contact, generally caused by therapeutic agents applied to the area), pityriasis versicolor (tinea versicolor), nummular eczema, erythema annulare centrifugum, and erythrasma (an eruption of reddish brown patches resulting from superficial bacterial skin infection caused by Corynebacterium minutissimum).

<details><summary>Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch): recall the key points for treatment — additional detail.</summary>

Oral terbinafine, itraconazole, and fluconazole are options but do not have a U.S. Food and Drug Administration indication for tinea cruris.
