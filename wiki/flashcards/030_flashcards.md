<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Chlamydia trachomatis is associated with a range of clinical manifestations, including neonatal conjunctivitis, nasopharyngitis, and pneumonia in young infants as well as genital tract infection, lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV), and trachoma in children, adolescents, and adults.

<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

C trachomatis is an obligate intracellular bacterial agent with at least 15 serologic variants (serovars) divided between the following biologic variants (biovars): oculogenital (serovars A–K) and LGV (serovars L1, L2, and L3). Trachoma usually is caused by serovars A through C, and genital and perinatal infections are caused by serovars B and D through K.

<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

C trachomatis is the most commonly reported notifiable condition in the United States, with highest rates among sexually active female1 adolescents (ages 15–24 years). A significant proportion of these patients are asymptomatic, providing an ongoing reservoir for infection. Among sexually active 15- to 24-year-olds participating in the 2015–2018 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the estimated 2018 incidence was 8,219 per 100,000 population among 15- to 24-year-old female individuals and 4,122 per 100,000 among 15- to 24-year-old male individuals.2 Among those tested for chlamydial infection through the STD Surveillance Network of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2020, the highest proportion of patients testing positive were women 19 years and younger (27.6%) and men 19 years and younger who have sex with women only (35.2%).3,4 Oculogenital serovars of C trachomatis can be perinatally transmitted to neonates during birth.

<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Among postpubertal individuals, C trachomatis nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are the most sensitive tests and are recommended for laboratory diagnosis. Commercial NAATs have been cleared by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for testing vaginal (provider or patient collected), endocervical, penile urethral, throat, and rectal swab specimens; first-catch urine specimens; and liquid cytology specimens.

<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

• Neonates with chlamydial ophthalmia neonatorum are treated with oral erythromycin base or ethylsuccinate (50 mg/kg/day in 4 divided doses daily) for 14 days. Data on azithromycin for treating neonatal chlamydial infection are limited; however, a short therapy course might be effective. Topical antibiotic therapy alone is inadequate for treating chlamydial ophthalmia neonatorum and is unnecessary when systemic treatment is administered.

<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Pregnancy Identification and treatment of people with C trachomatis genital tract infection during pregnancy and before delivery can prevent peripartum acquisition of disease in the infant. Preventive Services Task Force recommend that all pregnant people aged ≤24 years of age as well as those older than 24 years who are at increased risk for chlamydia (eg, those who have a new sex partner, more than one sex partner, a sex partner with concurrent partners, or a sex partner who has an STI) should be routinely screened for C trachomatis at the first prenatal visit. Pregnant people who remain at increased risk for chlamydial infection also should be retested during the third trimester to prevent postnatal complications in the birthing person and chlamydial infection in the neonate.

<details><summary>Chlamydia trachomatis: recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

• Neonatal chlamydial conjunctivitis is characterized by ocular congestion, edema, and discharge developing a few days to several weeks after birth and lasting for 1 to 2 weeks and sometimes longer. In contrast to trachoma (described below), scars and pannus formation (vascularization of the normally avascular cornea) are rare.
