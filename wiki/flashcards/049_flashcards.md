<details><summary>Epstein-Barr Virus Infections (Infectious Mononucleosis): recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Infectious mononucleosis is the most common presentation of primary symptomatic Epstein- Barr virus (EBV) infection. It manifests typically as fever, pharyngitis with or without petechiae and often with exudate, lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Atypical lymphocytosis and elevated hepatic aminotransferase concentrations are frequently found on laboratory evaluation.

<details><summary>Epstein-Barr Virus Infections (Infectious Mononucleosis): recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

EBV (also known as human herpesvirus 4) is a gamma herpesvirus of the Lymphocryptovirus genus and is the most common cause of infectious mononucleosis (>90% of cases).

<details><summary>Epstein-Barr Virus Infections (Infectious Mononucleosis): recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Humans are the only known reservoir of EBV, and approximately 90% of U.S. adults have been infected. Close personal contact usually is required for transmission. The virus is viable in saliva for several hours outside the body; the role of fomites in transmission is unknown.

<details><summary>Epstein-Barr Virus Infections (Infectious Mononucleosis): recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Infectious mononucleosis is primarily a clinical diagnosis. An absolute increase in atypical lymphocytes and mild elevations in hepatic aminotransferase concentrations during the second week of illness with infectious mononucleosis are additional characteristic but nonspecific findings. Confirmation of primary EBV infection depends on results of serologic testing.

<details><summary>Epstein-Barr Virus Infections (Infectious Mononucleosis): recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

There is no antiviral treatment approved for EBV infection. Patients suspected of having infectious mononucleosis should not receive ampicillin or amoxicillin, which may cause nonallergic morbilliform rashes in a proportion of patients with active EBV infection. Although therapy with shortcourse corticosteroids may have a beneficial effect on some acute symptoms, because of potential adverse effects their use is usually considered only for patients with marked tonsillar inflammation with impending airway obstruction, massive splenomegaly, myocarditis, hemolytic anemia, HLH, or severe thrombocytopenia.

<details><summary>Epstein-Barr Virus Infections (Infectious Mononucleosis): recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

EBV is associated with several other distinct disorders, including X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome, posttransplantation lymphoproliferative disorders, Burkitt lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, undifferentiated B- or T-lymphocyte lymphomas, and leiomyosarcoma. X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome occurs most often in people with an inherited, maternally derived, recessive genetic defect in the SH2DIA or XIAP/BIRC4 genes, which are important in several lymphocyte signaling pathways.
