<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Onchocerciasis primarily affects skin, subcutaneous tissues, and eyes. Subcutaneous, nontender nodules containing male and female adult worms can be up to several centimeters in diameter and are more common over bony prominences. Nodules tend to be found on the lower torso, pelvis, and lower extremities in patients infected in Africa, whereas in patients infected in South America, nodules are located more often on the upper body (the head and trunk).

<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

Onchocerca volvulus is a filarial nematode.

<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

O volvulus has no significant animal or environmental reservoir. Humans are infected when infectious larvae are transmitted through the bites of Simulium species flies (black flies). Black flies breed in fast-flowing streams and rivers (hence, the colloquial name for the disease, “river blindness”).

<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Direct microscopic examination of a 1- to 2-mm biopsy specimen of the epidermis and upper dermis (taken from the posterior iliac crest or other areas with highest suspicion for local microfilariae), incubated in saline, can reveal emerging microfilariae. Multiple skin snips may be needed to make a diagnosis, and in children in whom clinical suspicion of onchocerciasis is high, use of a nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) may increase diagnostic yield if microscopic examination is negative. Microfilariae are not found in blood.

*Printed p. 614 / PDF p. 628*
</details>

<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Ivermectin and moxidectin are approved in the United States for treatment of onchocerciasis, although moxidectin is not yet available commercially. Patients from areas with endemic Loa loa infection should be screened for L loa and should be referred to an expert if coinfected with O volvulus and L loa, as treatment of patients with high levels of circulating L loa microfilaremia with ivermectin rarely can result in fatal encephalopathy. Ivermectin can be used in younger children weighing more than 15 kg.

<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Repellents and protective clothing (long sleeves and pants) can decrease exposure to bites from black flies, which bite by day. Treatment of vector breeding sites with larvicides is effective for controlling black fly populations. Vector control, however, largely has been supplanted by community-wide mass ivermectin administration programs.

*Printed p. 615 / PDF p. 629*
</details>

<details><summary>Onchocerciasis (River Blindness, Filariasis): recall the key points for treatment — additional detail.</summary>

A 6-week course of doxycycline can be used in addition to ivermectin or moxidectin to kill adult worms through depletion of their endosymbiotic rickettsia-like bacteria Wolbachia, which appear to be required for survival of O volvulus. Doxycycline may be used regardless of age in nonpregnant patients to obviate the need for years of ivermectin treatment.
