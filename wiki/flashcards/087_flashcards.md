<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Invasive meningococcal infection usually results in septicemia (35%–40% of cases), meningitis (~50% of cases), or both. Bacteremic pneumonia is less common (10% of cases). Rarely, Neisseria meningitidis is identified as a cause of occult bacteremia.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

N meningitidis is a gram-negative diplococcus with 12 confirmed serogroups based on capsular type.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

N meningitidis disease rates are highest in infants <1 year of age, followed by children 1 to 4 years of age, the very elderly (≥85 years), and adolescents and young adults 16 to 23 years of age. Household contacts of cases have 500 to 800 times the rate of disease of the general population. A predominance of U.S. cases is observed in the winter, often noted 2 to 3 weeks following onset of influenza outbreaks, with a peak of cases in January, February, and March.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for diagnostic tests.</summary>

Cultures of blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are indicated for patients with suspected invasive meningococcal disease. Cultures of a petechial or purpuric lesion scraping, synovial fluid, and other usually sterile body fluid specimens sometimes are positive. Specimens for culture should be plated onto both sheep blood and chocolate agar and incubated at 35°C to 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide in a moist atmosphere.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

The priority in management of meningococcal disease is treatment of shock in meningococcemia and of raised intracranial pressure in severe meningitis. Empirical therapy for suspected meningococcal disease should include cefotaxime or ceftriaxone. Once the microbiologic diagnosis is established, treatment options include cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, penicillin G, or ampicillin.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for infection prevention and control measures in health care settings.</summary>

In addition to standard precautions, droplet precautions are recommended until 24 hours after initiation of effective antimicrobial therapy.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for control measures.</summary>

Care of Exposed People Postexposure Chemoprophylaxis Regardless of immunization status, close contacts including household contacts of all people with invasive meningococcal disease , whether endemic or in an outbreak situation, are at high risk of infection and should promptly receive chemoprophylaxis. Chemoprophylaxis should be provided even if the close contact has received a meningococcal vaccine. The decision to administer chemoprophylaxis to other contacts is based on risk of contracting invasive disease related to specific exposure to the secretions from the infected patient.

<details><summary>Meningococcal Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

The overall case fatality rate for invasive meningococcal disease is 15% and is somewhat higher in older adults. Clinical predictors of mortality include coma, hypotension, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia.
