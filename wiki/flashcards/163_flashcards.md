<details><summary>Varicella-Zoster Virus Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations.</summary>

Primary infection results in varicella (chickenpox), manifesting in unvaccinated people as a generalized, pruritic, erythematous vesicular rash typically consisting of 250 to 500 lesions in varying stages of development (papules, vesicles) and resolution (crusting), low-grade fever, and other systemic symptoms. Complications include bacterial superinfection of skin lesions with or without bacterial sepsis, pneumonia, central nervous system involvement (acute cerebellar ataxia, encephalitis, stroke/vasculopathy), thrombocytopenia, and rarer complications such as glomerulonephritis, arthritis, and hepatitis. Primary viral pneumonia is uncommon among immunocompetent children but is the most common complication in adults.

<details><summary>Varicella-Zoster Virus Infections: recall the key points for etiology.</summary>

VZV (also known as human herpesvirus 3) is a member of the Herpesviridae family, the subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae, and the genus Varicellovirus.

<details><summary>Varicella-Zoster Virus Infections: recall the key points for epidemiology.</summary>

Humans are the only source of infection for this highly contagious virus. Infection occurs when the virus comes in contact with the mucosa of the upper respiratory tract or the conjunctiva of a susceptible person. Person-to-person transmission occurs from contact with lesions from varicella or herpes zoster or from airborne spread.

Diagnostic tests for VZV are summarized in Table 163.1. Vesicular fluid or a scab can be used to identify VZV using a NAAT, which currently is the diagnostic method of choice. During the acute phase of illness, VZV also can be identified by a NAAT of saliva or buccal swab specimens, although viral DNA is more likely to be detected in vesicular fluid or scabs.

<details><summary>Varicella-Zoster Virus Infections: recall the key points for treatment.</summary>

Nonspecific therapies for varicella include keeping fingernails short to prevent trauma and secondary bacterial infection from scratching, frequent bathing, application of lotion to reduce pruritus, and acetaminophen for fever. Children with varicella should not receive salicylates or salicylatecontaining products (eg, aspirin, bismuthsubsalicylate), because these products increase the risk of Reye syndrome. Salicylate therapy should be stopped, if possible, in an unimmunized child who is exposed to varicella.

<details><summary>Varicella-Zoster Virus Infections: recall the key points for clinical manifestations — additional detail.</summary>

Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) establishes latency in sensory (dorsal root, cranial nerve, and autonomic including enteric) ganglia during primary VZV infection. This latency occurs with both wild-type VZV and the varicella vaccine strain.

<details><summary>Varicella-Zoster Virus Infections: recall the key points for diagnostic tests — additional detail.</summary>

A significant increase (fourfold rise in titer) in serum varicella immunoglobulin (Ig) G antibody between acute and convalescent samples by any standard serologic assay can confirm a diagnosis retrospectively, but this may not reliably occur in immunocompromised people. However, diagnosis of varicella by serologic testing seldom is indicated.
